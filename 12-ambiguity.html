Ambiguity of a grammar

A context-free grammar is ambiguous if some string in its language has two or more different parse trees. Equivalently, the string has two different leftmost derivations (or two different rightmost derivations).

Not the same thing

A leftmost and a rightmost derivation of the same tree are different derivations, but they do not show ambiguity. Ambiguity needs two different trees.

How to show a grammar is ambiguous

  1. Look for a place where the grammar offers a choice that does not change the string: a symbol that can be added on either side (S → aS | Sa), two operators without precedence (E → E + E | E * E), an optional else, an ε that can appear in several places, two branches that generate a common string.
  2. Choose the shortest string where that choice matters.
  3. Draw two parse trees for it and point out where they differ.

How to argue a grammar is not ambiguous

Show that at every step of a leftmost derivation the next production is forced by the string being derived: for example, only one variable is present and the length decides the production (grammar 3), or the structure of the grammar fixes precedence and associativity (grammar 10).
